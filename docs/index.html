Rocket Nozzle CFD: Compressible Flow Analysis

How does a rocket engine convert high-pressure combustion gases into thrust? The answer lies in the converging-diverging (de Laval) nozzle, a geometry that accelerates flow from subsonic to supersonic speeds. Understanding compressible flow through nozzles is fundamental to propulsion engineering.

What is a Converging-Diverging Nozzle?

A converging-diverging (CD) nozzle, also called a de Laval nozzle, is the fundamental geometry for accelerating gas flows to supersonic speeds. Invented by Swedish engineer Gustaf de Laval in 1888 for steam turbines, it now powers virtually every modern rocket engine.

The nozzle has three key regions: a converging section where subsonic flow accelerates, a throat (minimum area) where the flow reaches Mach 1 (sonic condition), and a diverging section where the flow continues to accelerate to supersonic velocities.

The physics is governed by compressible flow theory. As the cross-sectional area decreases in the converging section, the subsonic flow speeds up (similar to putting your thumb over a garden hose). At the throat, the flow becomes choked -- it cannot go faster by further reducing back pressure. In the diverging section, the supersonic flow behaves opposite to intuition: as area increases, velocity increases further, converting thermal energy into directed kinetic energy.

Compressible Flow & Shock Waves

Shock Diamonds

Shock diamonds (also called Mach diamonds) are the bright, repeating diamond-shaped patterns visible in rocket exhaust plumes. They form when the exhaust pressure at the nozzle exit does not match the ambient atmospheric pressure.

In overexpanded nozzles (exit pressure below ambient), the atmosphere compresses the exhaust, creating oblique shock waves that reflect off the free boundary. In underexpanded nozzles (exit pressure above ambient), the exhaust expands outward, creating expansion fans that also reflect. These reflections produce the characteristic diamond pattern.

The spacing and intensity of shock diamonds depend on the pressure mismatch, Mach number, and gas properties. They are most visible in the first few diameters downstream of the nozzle exit.

Overexpanded vs Underexpanded

The relationship between nozzle exit pressure (Pe) and ambient pressure (Pa) determines the exhaust plume structure:

  • ► Overexpanded (Pe < Pa): Ambient pressure pushes inward on exhaust. Oblique shocks form inside the nozzle or at exit. Common at sea level with high-expansion-ratio nozzles.
  • ► Underexpanded (Pe > Pa): Exhaust pressure exceeds ambient, causing plume to expand outward. Expansion fans at exit boundary. Common at high altitude or with low-expansion-ratio nozzles.
  • ► Perfectly expanded (Pe = Pa): Ideal condition with no shocks or expansion fans at exit. Maximum thrust efficiency. Difficult to achieve across all altitudes.

Real-World Rocket Engines

SpaceX Merlin 1D

The workhorse of the Falcon 9 first stage. An open-cycle gas generator engine burning RP-1 (kerosene) and liquid oxygen. With an expansion ratio of 16:1, it produces 845 kN of thrust at sea level. The Merlin family has evolved through multiple iterations, with the 1D variant achieving the highest thrust-to-weight ratio of any rocket engine in history.

RS-25 (Space Shuttle Main Engine)

One of the most powerful and sophisticated liquid-fuel rocket engines ever built. The RS-25 uses a staged-combustion cycle with liquid hydrogen and liquid oxygen, achieving an expansion ratio of 77:1 and chamber pressure of 20.6 MPa. Originally powering the Space Shuttle, it now serves as the core stage engine for NASA's Space Launch System (SLS).

RL10B-2 (Delta IV Upper Stage)

The RL10B-2 holds the record for highest specific impulse (Isp) of any operational rocket engine at 462 seconds. Its extreme expansion ratio of 285:1 is made possible by a carbon-carbon composite nozzle extension. Powered by liquid hydrogen and liquid oxygen, it is the upper stage engine for the Delta IV and now the Vulcan Centaur.

Why Computational Fluid Dynamics?

Experimental testing of rocket nozzles is expensive, dangerous, and time-consuming. CFD provides a complementary approach that offers several key advantages:

📈

Cost Reduction

A single hot-fire test of a large rocket engine costs millions of dollars. CFD simulations can explore hundreds of design variants at a fraction of the cost, narrowing the design space before physical testing.

🔍

Flow Visualization

CFD provides complete flow field data -- pressure, temperature, Mach number, and density at every point. Experimental measurements are limited to probe locations or surface measurements. CFD reveals shock structures, boundary layers, and separation regions that are difficult to measure experimentally.

⚙

Parametric Studies

CFD enables rapid parametric sweeps across expansion ratios, chamber pressures, and throat radii. This project sweeps three parameters to build an aerodynamic database, revealing how each variable affects performance and plume structure.

🛠

Design Optimization

Nozzle contour optimization (e.g., Rao bell design) requires evaluating many geometries. CFD allows rapid iteration on the Bezier control points that define the nozzle shape, optimizing for thrust coefficient, Isp, or weight.

📊

Validation Framework

Modern CFD codes like SU2 are validated against analytical solutions and experimental data. This project implements triple validation (isentropic theory, Method of Characteristics, and grid convergence) to establish confidence in the computational results.

🌎

Altitude Effects

CFD can simulate nozzle performance at any altitude by changing the back pressure boundary condition. This reveals how overexpansion and flow separation evolve from sea level to vacuum, informing nozzle design for specific mission profiles.
